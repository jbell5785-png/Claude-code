  1. Legend

    Legend has it…

    “…Henry VIII, King of England from 1509-1547, came across Anne Boleyn and her Maids of Honour (the young ladies who attended the Queen), eating the tarts from a silver dish. Tasting one for himself, the King was so delighted by its ‘melt-in-the-mouth’ sensation that he confiscated the recipe and demanded it be kept secret in a locked iron box at Richmond Palace.”

    One account goes further: the Maid who invented the tart “was imprisoned within the Palace grounds and ordered to produce the delectable tarts solely for King Henry and his royal household!” Richmond’s local historians add that a Maids of Honour recipe appears in R. May’s The Accomplisht Cook, 2nd edition, 1665.

Our story
Our story

Before Kew, there was Hill Street.

“Certainly by the early 18th century the recipe had been disclosed to a bakery in Richmond and the tasty little tarts became one of the features of fashionable Richmond…”

Who has kept the secret

  1. The Original Shop, Hill Street

    “The first Original Maids of Honour shop was on the corner of Hill Street in Richmond.” The postcard shows Billett’s name in the window.

  2. Robert Newens

    “Here a young lad called Robert Newens served an apprenticeship and went on to open his own premises, first in King Street and later at No 3 George Street…”

  3. Alfred Nashbar Newens

    His son opened “a brand new establishment on the Kew Road – exactly where we are today.” Est. 1850 to confirm

  4. John and Kathleen, then Peter

    Alfred left the business to his son John and daughter Kathleen in the 1920s. After severe bomb damage in the Second World War, John’s son Peter “set work to get the business back on its feet. The bake house was rebuilt with new gas ovens installed, and the shop front was remodelled.”

  5. Today

    “Today these delectable pastries are still served warm from our ovens…”
