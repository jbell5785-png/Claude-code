## 1. Only-here details, ranked by design potential

### 1. A café inside the vaults of London's oldest surviving Thames bridge
- **Evidence:**
  - Richmond Council's conservation appraisal (adopted 20 Nov 2023) says: "On the Richmond side of the bridge, there are business uses (e.g. café) in the converted arches beneath Richmond Bridge" `[S10]`.
  - The café's own site says it is "in a converted arch beneath Richmond Bridge" and "nestled in the arch beneath" `[S1][S4]`.
  - The bridge's listing text, as mirrored from Historic England list entry 1180951, says: "Now the oldest bridge over the Thames within the boundaries of Greater London" `[S9]`.
  - Grade I. The listing gives the date as 1777, the architects as James Paine and Kenton Couse, the material as Portland stone, and "Widened 1937" `[S9][S10]`.
- **Status:**
  - Café in the arches beneath the bridge: **VERIFIED**.
  - Grade I and oldest surviving bridge: **VERIFIED** via the council appraisal plus the listing-text mirror.
  - The Historic England page itself refused access (HTTP 403 to WebFetch, and to one default Playwright load) `[S8]`.
- **Dates:** Construction began on 23 Aug 1774. The bridge opened to pedestrians in Sept 1776 and to traffic on 12 Jan 1777, and was completed in Dec 1777. **REPORTED** (Wikipedia, citing Lysons 1810 and Matthews 2008) `[S11]`. The listing simply says "1777".
- **Design hook:** A cultural claim no other café can make: "the oldest Thames bridge in London is our ceiling." Use it in one place, stated precisely as "the oldest *surviving* Thames bridge in Greater London". Don't use "oldest bridge in London".

### 2. The five arches rising to the centre
- **Evidence:** The listing describes "Five moulded segmental arches, rising gradually to the centre, which has the widest and tallest arch", plus "Two land-bound semi-circular arches" and "Moulded semi-circular cutwaters" `[S9]`. Wikipedia gives a central span of about 60 ft (18 m) and a length of 300 ft `[S11]`. An 1811 source quoted on thames.me.uk gives the central arch as 25 ft high and 60 ft wide `[S13]`.
- **Status:**
  - Five arches rising to the centre: **VERIFIED** `[S9][S10]`.
  - Two land-bound arches: **VERIFIED** (listing text).
  - Dimensions: **REPORTED**.
- **Caution:** Nothing says which of the arches the café occupies. The café is brick-fronted and the bridge is Portland stone, so do **not** draw the café as one of the five river arches. See detail 4.
- **Design hook:** A real, countable rhythm: 5 arches, the centre one the widest and tallest. It can drive navigation, section dividers or a skyline band.

### 3. The tide at the door: a tidal river held at half-tide
- **Evidence:**
  - The Thames at Richmond is tidal. Richmond Lock and its half-tide weir, which opened on 19 May 1894, lie downstream.
  - Its three sluice gates are raised "for about two hours each side of the published time of high tide". The rest of the time they hold the reach up to Teddington at or above half-tide level. **VERIFIED**, via Wikipedia `[S18]` and PLA wording seen in search results `[S19]`.
  - The PLA site blocked both WebFetch and Playwright (Cloudflare check), so its pages could not be read directly `[S19]`.
  - Teddington is the usual tidal limit `[S18]`.
  - The council appraisal says the towpaths are "prone to flooding" and captions a photo "Friars Lane showing tidal flooding" `[S10]`. Walkers report tidal flooding of the Thames Path at Richmond Riverside, just north of the bridge, varying "with the state of the tide" `[S21]`.
- **Status:**
  - The café's own garden flooding: **UNKNOWN**. No source found.
  - The 2017 Thames Path article says you can sit outside on the downstream side "to watch the river and tide" `[S7]`. **REPORTED**.
- **Annual draw-off:** Each year the half-tide barrier is left open at low water for maintenance, and the riverbed is exposed `[S18][S20]`.
  - ianVisits (2 Oct 2026, citing PLA notice U35-26) gives 2026 as "1:30pm Thursday 5th November 2026 through to 10am Friday 18th November".
  - 18 Nov 2026 is a **Wednesday**, so the end date is uncertain. **REPORTED**, conflicting `[S20]`.
- **Design hook:** The strongest link to the name. The site can know the river's state.

### 4. The brick arch with a carved, bearded stone face as its keystone
- **Evidence:** A brick arch of radiating dark purple-brown and red bricks with pale mortar. At its crown is a carved **bearded male face** in pale grey weathered stone, with curling hair, set as the keystone. Below it is a full-width glazed arched screen with steel-framed glass double doors.
  - The café's own Gallery photo `thumbs_img_3037-1` `[S2]` (2018).
  - Client-supplied Google Maps photo (third-party), viewed 25 Oct 2026, "11 months ago" `[S24-3]`.
- **Status:** The face exists: **VERIFIED**, since it appears in two photos about 7 years apart.
- **What the face is:** **UNKNOWN**.
  - The bridge listing text `[S9]` does not mention masks, heads, keystones or the brick arches.
  - Searches of local and heritage sources (council appraisal, thames.me.uk, London Historians, Wikipedia) found nothing `[S10][S11][S12][S13]`.
  - Whether these brick arches are original 1777 work, part of the 1937–40 widening (when the approach ramps were raised `[S11]`), or a later riverside structure: **UNKNOWN**.
  - A 2013/2017 note by the bridge's historian says newer upstream brickwork from the widening can be told apart from "the original brickwork" when you look up under the arches `[S12]`. So the bridge does contain brick. That is not evidence about this arch.
  - Do **not** call the face "Father Thames", a "river god" or a "Green Man".
- **Related fact:** Terrace Gardens, home of the sister café, contains John Bacon's Coade stone statue of Father Thames, bought by the Duke of Montagu in 1781 `[S15]`. **REPORTED** (Wikipedia). This is a separate object, and any link between it and the face is unproven.
- **Design hook:** A face that greets every visitor and is unique to this door. It could become a quiet mark, such as a favicon or a footer sign-off, drawn new from scratch. It must not be traced from a photo. Ask the client what it is [TO CONFIRM].

### 5. Inside the vault: whitewashed brick, ferns, chalkboards, and a far door
- **Evidence:**
  - Client-supplied Google Maps photo (third-party), viewed 25 Oct 2026, "5 months ago" `[S24-1]`:
    - A long, narrow **barrel vault** of whitewashed brick, with older red-brown brick showing where the whitewash has worn, and a curved white sheet panel lining part of the vault.
    - Ferns and trailing plants in woven hanging baskets, track spotlights, and a red pendant shade.
    - Grey polka-dot oilcloth on the tables, mismatched wooden chairs, large pale tiles, and a long grey-panelled counter with wicker baskets and an espresso machine.
    - At the far end, a smaller brick arch and a glazed door with daylight.
  - The café's own 2018 Gallery photos `[S2]` show the same whitewashed vault, with globe wall lights, cakes under glass domes, a long chalkboard over the counter, and a hanging round sign reading "Hot food & drinks served here". Looking outward, the glazed arch frames the garden, with steps beyond.
- **Status:**
  - The vault and its finishes: **VERIFIED** (two photo sets, 2018 and about 2026).
  - Depth or length of the vault: **UNKNOWN**. No source gives it.
- **Design hook:** The page can *be* the tunnel. You scroll from the glazed river-end arch to the far door, and the curve of the whitewashed vault frames everything.

### 6. The skiff overhead
- **Evidence:** "A skiff hangs above the counter" `[S7]` (2017). The café's own Gallery photo `thumbs_img_3009` `[S2]` shows a long, slender wooden boat hull, with a red band and a pale underside, hanging along the vault.
- **Status:** **REPORTED** (2017), consistent with the café's 2018 photo. Whether it is still there: **UNKNOWN**. In the 2026 third-party photo that part of the vault shows a curved white panel, so it may have been removed [TO CONFIRM].
- **Design hook:** A rowing boat inside a bridge, right by the boat-hire steps. A light, physical motif. Use only once confirmed as current.

    - gravel underfoot;

    - black wire-mesh chairs, dark tables, dark green plastic chairs and wooden chairs, with food served on trays.
  - The café's own 2018 photo `thumbs_img_6870` `[S2]` shows the same gravel garden with green plastic chairs and a dog among the tables.
- **Status:**
  - The garden and plane trees: **VERIFIED**.
  - "Three" trees: **REPORTED** (the café's own claim).
  - The Discovery Richmond guide and a 2024 blog both mention the "large riverside garden" or "large, ancient trees" `[S22][S23]`.
- **Design hook:** A real canopy. Dappled shade, gravel texture and the stone arches behind can carry the summer state of the site.

### 10. Boats, steps and the tide-hire board next door
- **Evidence:**
  - The café's own 2018 photos `[S2]` show wooden rowing skiffs moored at the Richmond riverside steps, some under blue covers, with the towpath beside them.
  - Client-supplied Google Maps photo (third-party), viewed 25 Oct 2026, "8 years ago" `[S24-2]`: a boat-hire chalkboard, belonging to a separate business, partly hidden by a bicycle. It reads "HIGH WATER 20[…]", "Ride the tide", "LEAVE before H.W.", "RETURN after H.W.", "NON TIDAL […]", "All Boa[ts …]". The times are not legible.
  - The café says it is "just by the embarkation point for boats to Hampton Court, and for boats that head to the city during the summer months" `[S1]`. **REPORTED**, as the café's claim. Current operators and timetables: UNKNOWN.
  - The 2017 article: of the arches next to the café, "one is still a boat builders", and the café is the one "furthest inland" `[S7]`. **REPORTED** (2017).
- **Design hook:** Riverside life runs on high water ("H.W."). The local vocabulary of the tide, "Ride the tide" and "Leave before H.W.", is real and nearby. Use the idea, not the other business's board.

### 11. Through the towpath tunnel
- **Evidence:** Walking upstream along the towpath, "you can pass through the tunnel under the bridge", and the café is in one of the arches beside it `[S7]`. The listing records "Two land-bound semi-circular arches" `[S9]`.
- **Status:**
  - The tunnel: **REPORTED** `[S7]`.
  - That the towpath tunnel is one of the land-bound arches: an inference, **UNKNOWN**.
- **Design hook:** People arrive on foot from the Thames Path, not from a street. A "you're on the towpath" way in for the site.

### 12. Hollyhock Café and the walk through Grotto Gate
- **Evidence:**
  - The café's own site describes the sister café: "Hollyhock Café nestled in a quaint garden setting with river views" and "situated in the Terrace Gardens with a splendid view over the gardens and the River Thames". Its address is given as Hollyhock Cafe, Terrace Gardens, Richmond, Surrey TW10 6UX `[S1]`.
  - The 2017 Thames Path route `[S7]`: continue upstream from Richmond Bridge along the towpath, past the houses and gardens and before Petersham Meadows. Look for **Grotto Gate** low on the left. A tunnel leads to steps up into Terrace Gardens. The article also says Hollyhock has a veranda and shares the same menu.
  - Wikipedia says Grotto Gate is an 18th-century tunnel `[S15]`. The council says Terrace Gardens contains a café "open 7 days a week during daylight hours" with gender-neutral toilets, and that the gardens are "accessible at all times" `[S14]`.
  - Terrace Gardens is a Grade II registered park (ref. 1001551) `[S15]` (REPORTED, via Wikipedia). Wikipedia describes the café building as a "thatched tea-house" `[S15]` (REPORTED).
  - The café's own site invites you to "Stop in a[nd] ask for a free local map!" `[S1]`.
- **Status:**
  - The sister café, its location and same ownership: **VERIFIED** `[S1][S7]`.
  - The route: **REPORTED** (2017).
  - Walking distance and time: **UNKNOWN**.
  - The postcode conflicts. The café's site gives TW10 6UX `[S1]` and the council gives Terrace Gardens as TW10 6RH `[S14]`.
- **Design hook:** Two cafés joined by a riverside walk, a tunnel and a climb. The "free local map" is already part of how the café works, so a drawn map belongs here.

  - "All our milk is organic, too."

### 15. Toll-free since 1859
- **Evidence:** The bridge charged tolls, from ½d for a pedestrian, until it became toll-free on 25 March 1859. The toll houses were replaced by seating in 1868. Its bicentenary was celebrated on 7 May 1977.
  - **REPORTED**, from Wikipedia `[S11]` citing Cookson 2006 and Crisp 1866.
  - The bicentenary date is **VERIFIED** by the council appraisal `[S10]`.
- **Design hook:** A small history note: "a pedestrian once paid ½d to cross above us". Only for footnotes, and only cite the 1859 date with its sources.

**The name "Tide Tables".** No source explains where the name came from. Its origin is **UNKNOWN**; ask the client. Facts that can be stated:
- The Port of London Authority publishes annual tide tables for the tidal Thames, and runs tide gauges including one at Richmond. **REPORTED**: seen in PLA search results and walkers' pages `[S19][S21]`, but the PLA pages themselves could not be loaded.
- The river outside the café is tidal, regulated by the Richmond half-tide barrier `[S18]`.
- Do not claim the café publishes or displays tide tables.
- The 2017 article calls it both "Tide Tables" and "Tide Times" `[S7]`. The official name is **Tide Tables Café** `[S1][S17]`.

## 3. Signature ideas (only possible here)

Each idea is tied to a detail labelled above. Anything that displays tide data must use **real** PLA predictions or live gauge data, under licence or by permission. Otherwise label it clearly as a sample.

### 1. "Ride the tide" waterline (details 3 and 10)
- A thin waterline runs across the hero, drawn against a cut-out of the arch. It sits higher or lower according to today's predicted tide at Richmond, with the next high water time ("H.W. 14:12") in chalk-style type.
- Real mechanics:
  - Richmond's sluice gates are up for about 2 hours either side of high water `[S18]`.
  - The waterline could carry a small state label: "Gates up: river running free" within that window, and "Held at half-tide" outside it.
  - During the annual draw-off (2026 dates per `[S20]`, end date to confirm), the line drops to the riverbed with a one-line note.
- **Data:** PLA tide tables or Richmond gauge, permission needed [TO CONFIRM]. Without it, show a fixed, labelled sample.

### 2. Five arches as navigation (detail 2)
- Five arched tabs, the centre one widest and tallest, as the listing describes `[S9]`.
- The centre arch holds the most important page ("Visit / Today"). The outer arches step down for Menu, Garden, Hollyhock and Hire.
- A footnote: "Five arches, rising to the centre. Richmond Bridge, 1777. Grade I."
- Don't claim the café is one of the five.

### 3. Walk the vault (details 4–6)
- A vertical scroll that moves you through the whitewashed barrel vault.
- You enter under the newly drawn keystone face and glazed arch, pass the counter and chalkboards (the menu appears here as chalk), and reach the far door with daylight.
- Optional: the skiff glides overhead once at the counter section, but only if its current presence is confirmed (detail 6).

### 4. The Grotto Gate map (detail 12)
- An illustrated route map, pointedly "the free local map", from Tide Tables upstream along the towpath, past the houses, to Grotto Gate, through the tunnel and up the steps to Hollyhock in Terrace Gardens.
- Both cafés sit on one river line.
- Distances and times only after measuring [TO CONFIRM]. Grotto Gate and the route wording come from `[S7][S15]`.

### Optional seasonal state: "Under the planes" / "Blanket weather" (details 7–8)
- The garden band switches between dappled plane-tree shade in summer and a blanket-basket note in winter.
- Use it only after the client confirms the blankets are still offered.
