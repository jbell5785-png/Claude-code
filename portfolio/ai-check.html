The AI visibility check

What does Google’s AI tell your customers about you?

People ask Google and ChatGPT about you before they open your website: when you’re open, what things cost, whether you take bookings, whether the dog can come. I check what those answers say against what you actually publish, and show you where they’re wrong.

How it works

How the check works.

  1. Ask what customers ask Opening hours, the menu and prices, booking and cancellation terms, dietary needs, dogs, and the dish you’re known for.
  2. Search like a local On Google, on a phone, from nearby, and in ChatGPT. I note what each answer says and where it says it got it.
  3. Check every answer Against your own website, menus and booking terms. Right, half right, missing or wrong.
  4. Show you A short written report with screenshots: what’s wrong, where the wrong answer came from, and what to fix first.

What it gets wrong.

  • “Doesn’t enforce a no-show or cancellation fee.”A Japanese restaurant whose booking terms do include a no-show charge. Google cited a forum thread.
  • Prices from 2024.Taken from an old takeaway PDF still sitting on the restaurant’s server.
  • A lunch minimum spend, applied to afternoon tea.A tearoom whose menus were photos, so the teas were priced from a gift-voucher shop instead.
  • No opening hours at all.A café with no hours set on its Google profile, and two official websites.
  • “Strictly walk-in” and “very dog friendly”.Repeated from a years-old answer on a review site, not from the café.
  • The wrong discount days.A collection discount that runs every day, described as Tuesday to Thursday and Friday lunch.

The blind test

Accuracy, not rankings.

Does a better website change the answers?

For three of the places above, I asked AI search tools the same ten customer questions twice: once with the business’s current web pages, and once with my redesign in their place. Then I checked every answer against the business’s own published facts, without knowing which version it came from.

The caveats, in full

  • It’s small. Three places, ten questions each, one run per version.
  • It’s simulated. The answers came from a fixed copy of what the web says about each business, with my redesign swapped in for their current site. Search results and other websites weren’t refreshed, as they would be over time in real life.
  • Scores depend on the marking. The tearoom was marked twice: the stricter, fairer re-run is shown. Compare the two numbers in a row, not one row with another.
  • It measures accuracy, not ranking. It says nothing about where anyone appears in search results.
  • Nobody can guarantee rankings, in Google or in ChatGPT, and I won’t. Google itself says no special optimisation is needed to appear in its AI answers. What a clear website does is give the right facts a better chance of being repeated.

What changes

What a website fixes, and what it doesn’t.

A clear website changes

  • One current menu, as real text, with prices.
  • Hours and booking terms in plain words.
  • Structured data that matches what’s on the page.
  • A short summary file for AI tools (llms.txt).
  • Old PDFs retired, so stale prices stop being quoted.

It doesn’t change on its own

  • Your Google Business Profile.
  • Listings on other sites.
  • Reviews.
  • Ads that others place on your name.

That’s what the “get found” setup is for. You stay the owner of your Google profile, and there are no fake reviews and no keyword stuffing.
