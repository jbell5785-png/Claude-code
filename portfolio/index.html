The swap test

Watch a template turn into a place.

Most restaurant websites could belong to anyone. Change the name and nothing else needs to change. That’s the test I use on my own work: if a design still works with another name on it, it fails.

  1. The template A stock photo, “Welcome to our restaurant”, a PDF menu. Swap the name and it still works, so it fails.
  2. Mark it up What could be anywhere? What’s only here? I go and look, and take notes.
  3. Redraw it From the place itself: here, a café under a brick arch by the river, with a face carved on the keystone.
  4. The result A menu in real text, an honest way to call or find them, and a page that stops making sense with any other name on it.

Four places, studied and drawn.

Each is a speculative redesign for an independent business in and around Richmond. The owners haven’t agreed to be shown yet, so here they’re described by type, not by name. For each one I drew three different directions; below is the idea I’d lead with.

A neighbourhood Italian restaurant

The room I used to work in

Only here
A line of shirts strung across the dining room, over the tables.
The idea
Every section of the site hangs from a washing line, in the dining room’s own colours.
Signature
The shirts are the navigation. They sway as you scroll and swing back when you tug them.
What it fixes
One menu as real text, one current offer instead of two pages that disagree, and booking that hands over to their existing system without faking a confirmation.

A family-run Japanese restaurant

Only here
Twin awnings on a corner, a long narrow room, a stepped oak slat wall, and a printed menu with its own flame-seared dishes.
The idea
Their printed menu card comes online for the first time, in its own colours, rules and bullets.
Signature
A small torch sears, letter by letter, the names of the dishes their own menu calls seared. The other directions roll the awning up like a blind, or turn the slat wall into the navigation.
What it fixes
Google’s AI told diners there was no no-show policy (there is), quoted prices from an old PDF and gave the wrong discount days. The concept puts booking terms in plain words and the current menu in real text.

A historic tearoom and bakery

Only here
A painted sign on the side wall listing what they serve, signwritten boards with a gable over the door, and an old legend about a recipe kept in a locked iron box.
The idea
The whole site is signwritten like the building: painted boards, gold lettering, a gable on every heading.
Signature
The wall sign keeps London time. The line being served right now is gilded, and each line opens its own menu and books its own sitting. The other directions open the iron box with a key (told as legend), or fill a three-tier stand as you choose a tea.
What it fixes
Their menus were photos, so Google’s AI priced the afternoon teas from a gift-voucher shop and applied the lunch minimum spend to afternoon tea. In the concept, every set tea is real text with its own price and terms.

Blind test 10 → 18 out of 20 read the caveats

A riverside café under a bridge arch

Only here
A brick arch with a carved face at the keystone, a whitewashed vault hung with ferns and festoon lights, and the river at the door.
The idea
You enter the website the way you enter the café, and every page after that lives inside the vault.
Signature
The face at the keystone looks down as you approach, the doors open, and scrolling walks you down the vault to the garden and the river. The other directions use the bridge’s arches as navigation, or a tide that rises over the steps as you scroll.
What it fixes
No opening hours on their Google profile, two official websites, and no published menu, so a scraper site wrote one for them. The concept gives one site, hours in their own words and a menu layout ready for the real one.

Method

Same steps every time. Different answer every time.

How I work.

  • Look first

    Every concept starts from something only that place has: the building, the room, an object or a ritual. I go and look before I draw anything.

  • The swap test

    Put another restaurant’s name on the design. If it still works, I start again.

  • Draw it

    Original illustration instead of stock photos, so the site looks like your place, because it’s drawn from it.

  • Menus as real text

    Not a PDF and not a photo, so customers can read it on a phone and search engines can read your dishes and prices.

  • Honest booking

    The site hands over to the booking system, phone line or email you already use. It never pretends a table is booked.

  • Check what AI says

    I check what Google’s AI tells customers about you against what you actually publish, and fix the gaps at the source.

The AI check

Accuracy, not rankings. Nobody can promise rankings.

What does Google’s AI tell your customers?

Customers now ask Google and ChatGPT “are they open on Monday?” or “do they take bookings?” and get an answer without opening your website. I check those answers against what you publish. The usual culprits are old PDFs, menus posted as photos, and forum posts from years ago.

Read this before the numbers. It’s a small, simulated test: I asked AI search tools the same ten customer questions with each business’s current web pages and then with my redesign in their place, and checked every answer against the business’s own published facts without knowing which version it came from. It shows how accurate the answers were, not where anyone ranks. Nobody can guarantee rankings, and I won’t.
